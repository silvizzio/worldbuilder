Camera controller settings decide how viewers move through a scene and what they see the moment it opens.

For a shared project, the opening view carries more weight than any other single setting. A reviewer who receives a link has no briefing and no context. What loads on screen is their entire introduction to the project, and it determines whether they understand the scope in five seconds or spend a minute navigating to work out what they are looking at.

## Field reference

| Field | Description | Recommended usage |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Controls | The navigation controller type used in the 3D scene. | Match the controller to the project type and the expected viewing behaviour. |
| Initial Camera Position | Where the camera sits when the scene opens. | Place it where viewers immediately understand the main scene context. |
| Initial Camera Rotation | Which direction the camera faces when the scene opens. | Aim at the most important area, model, or spatial relationship. |
| Initial Target LookAt | The point the camera is oriented toward. | Use with camera position to create a stable, readable opening view. |
| Camera FOV | Field of view and perspective strength. | Tune alongside the initial view to balance scene coverage against visual focus. |

## Set controller type

Use **Controls** to change the controller according to the scenario.

| Scenario | Suits | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Object and map scenes | Orbit-style third person | The subject is examined from outside, so viewers circle it rather than enter it |
| Space scenes | First person walkthrough | Interiors are understood by moving through them at human scale |

Project type sets a sensible default at creation, but it does not lock the choice. Change it when the scene's actual use differs from its category: a large object that viewers need to walk around inside, for example, or an interior presented as a cutaway rather than a walkthrough.

## Set initial camera view

Adjust the **Initial** camera parameters to define what viewers see when the scene loads.

Treat this as the project's opening frame. It should present the most important area clearly and orient the viewer before they start navigating. Position, rotation, and target work together: position places the camera, rotation and target decide what it addresses.

A useful test is to reload the scene and look at the first frame as though you had never seen the project. If it takes more than a moment to work out what the scene contains and where the subject is, the opening view needs work.

This matters most for shared projects, where external reviewers rely entirely on the first view to understand scope.

## Set controller FOV

Adjust **Camera FOV** to control field of view, scene coverage, and perspective strength.

A wider FOV shows more surrounding context and exaggerates depth, which suits scenes where spatial relationships matter more than individual detail. A narrower FOV concentrates attention and flattens perspective, which suits presenting a single asset without distortion.

Review FOV together with the initial view rather than in isolation. The two combine to determine whether the opening frame reads naturally: a well-placed camera at the wrong FOV can still produce an opening that feels distorted or claustrophobic.

## Why this matters

Camera configuration is where a technically correct scene becomes a comprehensible one. The reconstruction can be flawless and the assets perfectly placed, and a poor opening view will still leave a reviewer confused about what they are being shown.

It is also the cheapest thing to fix and the easiest to forget, because the person configuring it has been looking at the scene for hours and no longer sees it fresh.
